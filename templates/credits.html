About & credits

We Elect exists to renew a sense of civic responsibility and a healthy public square: know who speaks for you, read the strongest case on every side, and decide for yourself.

We Elect helps you find the officials who represent you. It is an independent, nonpartisan project and is not affiliated with any government agency, political party or campaign. Information may be out of date; confirm with official sources.

How we write both sides

We Elect is a Brunner Creative site. The “Both sides” summaries are editorial content that summarize arguments made by others. They are not the opinion of We Elect, they do not say who is right, and they carry no ratings.

  • Choosing votes. We pick recent, recorded roll-call votes on widely covered bills, aiming for a mix of House and Senate and of topics, and for votes with some bipartisan support where possible. We do not choose votes by which party they help or hurt.
  • Same format, same weight. Every item has a plain-language description of what the bill did, then “Strongest case for” and “Strongest case against,” always in that order, each 2–4 sentences. Each side is written the way its own supporters would recognize it. No party colors are used on the arguments.
  • What neither side is saying. Below the two sides, each item has a full-width section of two to four short, sourced facts that the arguments above do not address. A point is included only if (1) it concerns what the bill does, what it costs, how it is carried out, or who it affects; (2) none of the for or against arguments we cite already covers it; (3) it comes from a source we opened and read, preferring statute text, CBO, CRS, GAO and inspectors general; and (4) it can be stated without saying whether the bill is good or bad or what Congress should do. Where estimates differ or data are missing, we say so. We never pick a point because it helps or hurts a party. This section is editorial context, not the opinion of We Elect, and carries the same last-reviewed date as the rest of the item.
  • Sourcing rules. Every claim is tied to a numbered source that we opened and read. We prefer primary records (Congress.gov and CRS summaries, House and Senate roll calls, committee statements) and add think tanks, advocacy groups and news outlets from different perspectives. We attribute arguments to the people or groups who make them, quote only exact words, and take numbers from the source cited. If sources disagree, or we cannot verify a claim, we say so or leave it out. Nothing is presented as fact unless a source supports it.
  • Dates and updates. Each item shows when it was last reviewed (currently {{ bs_meta.last_reviewed }}). Analyses cited may be updated after our review date.
  • Corrections. If you think a fact is wrong or a side is missing its strongest argument, {% if corrections_email %}please write to {{ corrections_email }}.{% else %}please contact Brunner Creative, the publisher of this site; a dedicated corrections address will be posted here once available.{% endif %}

How we write the ballot-measure summaries

The pages under Ballot measures are editorial content that summarize arguments made by others. They are not the opinion of We Elect, they do not say how to vote, and they carry no ratings or endorsements.

  • Choosing measures. We cover the most widely debated statewide measures first, drawn from the statewide list on your results page (built from Ballotpedia), aiming for a mix of topics and of the most populous states. We do not choose measures by which party or side they help. We cover only some of the statewide measures; where there is no summary, the results page links to Ballotpedia and the state’s own ballot materials.
  • What a yes and no vote do. We take the plain-language meaning of a yes or no vote from the state’s official ballot materials (the Secretary of State, election department, voter guide or ballot-question text) wherever available. If the official wording is long, we show it as written.
  • Cost. The fiscal estimate comes from the state’s official analyst where one exists (for example the California Legislative Analyst’s Office, Nevada’s Legislative Counsel Bureau, Virginia’s Department of Planning and Budget). Where the two sides cite different numbers, we say so and show the official estimate.
  • Both sides. The strongest case for and the strongest case against, in the same order, with roughly equal length (we check that neither side is more than about 20% longer). Each side is attributed to named supporters and opponents, taken from the official ballot arguments where the state publishes them, or from named people and groups quoted by Ballotpedia and news outlets. We quote exact words only; before a measure is published, every quotation and every dollar or percentage figure is checked against the source it cites. Official ballot arguments are the opinions of their authors and are not checked for accuracy by the state; we attribute them rather than endorse them.
  • What neither side is saying. Included only where there is a sourced fact that bears on what the measure does and that none of the cited arguments covers, using the same rules as for key votes.
  • Campaign money. Totals are the contributions Ballotpedia had processed from state campaign-finance reports as of the date shown, and change daily. We name a donor only where the cited source does.
  • Dates and updates. Each page shows when it was last reviewed (currently {{ ms_meta.last_reviewed }}). Court rulings, new filings and campaign reports can change after that date.

How we wrote “How to get involved in local politics”

The get-involved page is general civic information, not advice on how to vote or whom to support. Every factual statement on it is drawn from a page we opened and read, listed at the bottom of that page: U.S. government sites (USAGov, the Election Assistance Commission), state and city government pages used only as examples, and nonpartisan references (Ballotpedia, the League of Women Voters Education Fund’s VOTE411, the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, CAPLAW and the National Conference of State Legislatures). We use no statistics, and because laws and processes vary by place we point you to your own clerk or election office for the details that apply to you.
